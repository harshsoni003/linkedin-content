# Visual brief

The image is often the most-seen and least-briefed part of a post. Most people finish the writing, then grab whatever is nearby: a stock graphic, abstract AI art, or a vague mood. On a post that collects leads through a comment word, the image carries the ask itself, which makes it too important to leave to chance. This skill writes the instructions so precisely that whoever makes the image knows what it must prove, show, and say.

**When to use:** the moment a finished post needs an image, before briefing a designer or prompting an image tool.
**Time it takes:** about 3 minutes.
**What you end with:** a one-page image brief: the main proof, the structure, every on-image word written out, the ask placement, and crop notes.

## What to give it

**1. The finished post, full text.** Good input looks like this:

> Write the visual brief for this post:
> The 23-point check we run on every new client's books.
> Last month it caught $48,000 in unfiled sales tax a brand didn't know it owed.
> [the rest of the post]

**2. If the post collects leads with a comment word: the word.**

> Comment word: "BOOKS"

**3. Optional but powerful: what real things you could screenshot.** The brief is only as strong as the real documents, dashboards, or results available.

> I have: the actual checklist in Google Docs, our client dashboard, the before and after P&L.

## The method

### Step 1. Name what the image must prove

One sentence. Prove, not illustrate.

If the post claims a system produced a month of dated posts, the image proves it by showing the dated month. A stock graphic of a robot proves nothing and wastes the post's most checkable space.

The strongest images are screenshots of the real thing. A real document. A real dashboard with real rows. Real output. Slightly imperfect and real beats polished and generic every time.

### Step 2. Pick the main proof

Name the single number, document, dashboard, or screenshot the image is built around. Choose one. An image with four competing numbers has no clear focus.

### Step 3. Name the layout by structure, never by mood

"Warm and premium" is not a brief. Structures that work:

- **Hero screenshot with a callout.** The real document or dashboard, with one thing circled.
- **Numbered step track.** Three to five steps, each a short card.
- **Before and after, split.** Only when both sides are real.
- **Single stat block.** One number at scale, one line of context.

Say which structure and why, and name the runner-up you rejected. That one sentence saves a round of revisions later.

### Step 4. Write every on-image word, word for word

Every word that appears on the image, written out. Not a description of the copy, the copy. Include the headline, every label, any caption, and the ask line. Nobody should be inventing text at design time.

### Step 5. Place the ask

Only for posts that collect leads with a comment word. If this post has no ask, skip this step and say so; the image just carries the proof.

The ask lives in the visual. Specify the comment word in quotes and where it sits: usually the bottom third, at the second-largest size on the asset after the headline.

**One ask, not four.** Every extra demand stacked on top, follow, repost, like, blurs the one instruction you actually need followed. Give the reader one clear action.

### Step 6. Format and crop

- Aspect ratio and pixel size. Use 4:5 portrait at 1080 x 1350 when the content needs vertical room; 1080 x 1080 square is the simpler alternative.
- What must survive a center crop, since feeds crop.
- Whether the headline is legible at one-third size.

## Output

1. What the image proves, one sentence.
2. The main number, document, dashboard, or screenshot.
3. Layout, named by structure, with the rejected runner-up.
4. Every word of on-image text.
5. The comment word and its placement, or "no ask, proof only".
6. Dimensions and crop-safe notes.

## What good looks like

Hand the brief to someone who has never read the post and ask them to make the image. If they can do it without asking a single question, the brief is done. And look at what it describes: if that image could sit on top of anyone else's post in your industry, it is illustrating, not proving. Rerun step 1.

## Guardrails

- Never describe a mood instead of a structure.
- Never put more than one main number on the image.
- Never stack multiple asks.
- Never fabricate a screenshot of something that does not exist. If the document or dashboard is not real yet, build it first, then take the screenshot.
- On a lead post, never leave the ask out of the image. The image is what travels when the text gets cut short.
- If the post has no real number, document, dashboard, or screenshot to show, say so plainly. A text-only post beats a decorative image.
