# Post writer

Most people hand AI a topic and get back a post that sounds impressive and says nothing, with a suspicious statistic in line three that nobody can defend. Then they spend an hour rewriting it, or worse, they publish it. The problem starts with the input: a topic invites the AI to invent, while a real moment gives it something to write. This skill takes one true thing from your week plus a description of how you write, and builds the post from only that. Where a real number is missing, it leaves a marked blank instead of making one up.

**When to use:** whenever you have something real to say and want the LinkedIn post written for you.
**Time it takes:** about 5 minutes.
**What you end with:** one draft with any missing facts marked, plus three alternate opening lines. It is ready for your final decision after you confirm those facts.

## What to give it

**1. The idea: 1 to 3 sentences about something real.** A thing that happened, a number that moved, an opinion you would defend, a mistake you watched someone make. A moment, not a topic.

> Weak (a topic): "Something about cash flow for small businesses."
> Strong (a moment): "New client came in yesterday, nobody had reconciled their books in 14 months. Their bookkeeper was a cousin with a spreadsheet. They'd been paying for accounting software the whole time and nobody ever logged in."

**2. Your voice.** Paste your voice profile from voice-match if you have one. If not, paste one post or email that sounds like you and say "this is my voice." If you have neither, the post gets written clean and plain and labeled as neutral voice, so you know to edit it toward yourself.

**3. Optional: your goal for this post.** One of four: gain relevant followers, build trust with buyers, collect leads, start sales conversations. The goal shapes the ending. No goal given means the post ends on its strongest natural note.

## The method

1. **Find the post inside the idea.** What happened, what it cost or made, what changed, and what the reader should believe or do differently now. If a detail the post depends on is missing, keep writing and leave a marked blank. Never invent it, and never stall the user with a round of questions.
2. **Write a concise opening that works before LinkedIn's "see more."** The visible preview varies by device and layout. The opening has one job: open a question the reader has to keep reading to close. Five opening shapes that work:
   - A real result that makes the reader ask how.
   - A statement that contradicts something the reader believes.
   - A specific number with a twist the reader does not expect.
   - A scene already in motion, resolution withheld.
   - A challenge to how the reader currently does their work. Never a challenge to their intelligence.
3. **Obey the two hard rules of the opening.** First, the question must not answer itself before the cut: if the reader can guess the ending from the visible lines, the post dies right there. Second, never open and close the loop in one breath: "Most bookkeepers miss deadlines because they're disorganized. Get organized." resolves itself before the body starts.
4. **Add the second line, which deepens line one instead of repeating it.** It must add new information. Raise the stakes, reverse the expected explanation, make the cost concrete, or widen the scope. If deleting line two changes nothing, it is repetition. It stays on the same subject as line one and never contradicts it.
5. **Write the first line after "see more," under 10 words.** Re-promise the payoff: "Here's what that actually cost." Never spend this line on background or setup.
6. **Write the body in their voice.** The craft that holds no matter whose voice it is:
   - Conclusion early. The strongest thought does not wait until paragraph four.
   - Scene before principle. Let the reader picture the event, then hand them the rule.
   - Concrete beats abstract. Name the product, the price, the job title, the software. "A $340 invoice" works harder than "a small charge."
   - Vary sentence length. A long explaining sentence followed by a short landing line reads human. Ten identical short lines read like a robot doing intensity.
   - Numbers where they exist. Winning posts carry real numbers on far more of their lines than losing posts do, but forcing a digit onto every line reads like a spreadsheet. Use the numbers the user gave, and no others.
   - Every missing specific becomes a marked blank: [YOUR NUMBER: what the software costs per month] or [YOUR DETAIL: roughly when this happened]. Blanks are a feature. An invented number that gets challenged in the comments costs far more than a blank ever will.
7. **Land the ending on the goal.** Followers: end on the insight worth following for. Trust with buyers: end showing how you think or work, no pitch. Leads: point at the thing once, plainly, and only if the user actually has one. Conversations: end on one direct question a reader can answer in one line. Never end on a motivational poster, and never on "Agree?"
8. **Run the self-check before delivering.** Match the supplied voice profile, including its punctuation. No "It's not X, it's Y" constructions. No three-item list in every paragraph. No two consecutive lines built on the same skeleton. Sentence lengths vary. The opening stays concise before "see more." Every number came from the user. Every blank is marked.
9. **Write three alternate openings,** each in a different shape from the one used, each shown as its first two lines with the shape named and one line on when to prefer it. Then say which opening to run. A menu with no recommendation is not finished work.

## Output

Four parts, in this order:

**The post.** Ready to copy. Blanks marked inline as [YOUR NUMBER: ...] or [YOUR DETAIL: ...].

**Fill in before posting.** The list of blanks and what goes in each, or the line "Nothing. Every fact in this post came from you."

**Three alternate openings.** Each one: the first two lines, the shape it uses, and one line on when to prefer it.

**The call.** One line: which opening to run, and why.

## What good looks like

Read the first two lines alone, with the rest covered. If you already know exactly how the post ends, the opening gave the answer away: ask for new openings. Then scan the body for any fact you do not remember giving. Finding one means it was invented, and it gets replaced with a marked blank, not published.

## Guardrails

- Never invent a number, client, quote, event, or result. Missing specifics become marked blanks, never plausible filler.
- Never resolve the opening's question inside the visible lines.
- Never open with "Most people think", "Here's what", "I have a confession", or any line that could start anyone's post.
- No generic intensifiers. "Insane", "massive", "game-changing" create no stakes; the specific fact does.
- Match the supplied voice profile. If it says the writer avoids em dashes, replace them. If the samples use them consistently, preserve that habit. If there is no evidence, prefer a period or comma.
- Never pad. If the idea supports 120 words, deliver 120 good words, not 300.
- Never bolt a sales pitch onto a story. The goal shapes the ending; it does not hijack the body.
- Never deliver options without a recommendation.
- One post per idea. If the idea contains two posts, say so and write the stronger one.
