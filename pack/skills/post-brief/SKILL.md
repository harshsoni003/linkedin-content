# Post brief

The draft is the worst place to make decisions. Most founders open a blank page and start typing, so every choice that matters, who the post is for, what it claims, how it opens, gets made mid-sentence at 11pm. That is why drafts get rewritten four times and get worse each round. This skill locks the decisions first, so writing the post becomes execution instead of exploration.

**When to use:** right before writing any LinkedIn post, or when a draft keeps getting reworked without improving, which almost always means a decision upstream was never made.
**Time it takes:** about 5 minutes.
**What you end with:** a one-page brief: your goal, the hook family, five hooks with a clear pick, the second line, the one proof point, the target length, and every shaky claim caught before it ships.

## What to give it

**1. Your idea, 1 to 3 sentences about something real.**

Example of good input:

> "We stopped billing hourly in January and moved every client to flat monthly pricing. Revenue per client went up 22% and invoice complaints went to zero. Two peers told me clients would leave. One did."

**2. Your business goal for this post. Pick one:**

- Gain relevant followers
- Build trust with buyers
- Collect leads
- Start sales conversations

Example: "Build trust with buyers."

**3. Optional: your voice profile.** If you have your voice profile from voice-match, paste it. If not, paste one post that sounds like you. The brief works without either; the hooks just come out more generic.

## The method

### Step 1. Lock the business goal

One goal per post. A post chasing two goals achieves neither. The goal shapes every choice that follows:

| Goal | What it changes |
|---|---|
| Gain relevant followers | Take the widest true version of the idea. Hooks lean corrective or story. No ask at the end; the post itself is the ask. |
| Build trust with buyers | Take the version with your fingerprints on it: what you decided, what it cost, what happened. Hooks lean first person. End without a pitch; the proof does the selling. |
| Collect leads | The post points at something the reader can get. The hook says what that thing is worth, in numbers. End with the exact word to comment. |
| Start sales conversations | Narrow the reader on purpose. Name the exact situation only your buyer is in right now. End with a question or a soft door to your DMs. Smaller reach is fine and expected. |

Write one line: the goal, and what picking it just ruled out.

### Step 2. Pick the hook family, and say why

Not the hook. The family.

| Family | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| First person | Opens with a thing you did. "I built", "I spent", "I stopped", "We fired" |
| Bare product title | Names the thing you made, with no verb about you. "The 2026 pricing guide is here" |
| Bare number first | Opens on a number with no verb. "34 clients." |
| This X / Your X | Speaks straight at something the reader owns. "This clause is in your contract", "Your first hire" |
| Corrective | States a surprising truth against a common belief |
| Tool as subject | The tool does the acting. "Claude can now..." |
| Unfinished story | Opens inside a moment with the ending withheld. "The email came in at 11pm." |

State the reason this family fits this idea and this goal, in one sentence. If the reason is only "it usually works", the family and the idea are mismatched and the post will read as forced.

### Step 3. Five hooks in that family

Each one:

- 8 to 15 words
- Carries a concrete detail from your material. Use a real number when one is relevant. An opinion hook may work without a number when it states a specific, defensible belief and does not present that belief as a measured result.
- Makes a claim the body can actually pay off
- Stays concise enough to work before LinkedIn's "see more"; the visible preview varies by device and layout

Then name the strongest one and why, in one line. Five options with no recommendation is not a brief, it is a menu.

### Step 4. The second line

Line two is where most posts die, and it dies in one specific way.

**The second line must carry the hook's last beat forward.** If the hook ends on a count, line two continues on that count. If the hook ends on a price, line two stays with the price. A second line that changes subject leaves the reader holding an unanswered question, and they leave.

It must add information. If you could delete line two and nothing changes, it is repetition wearing a costume.

It must not contradict the hook. If the hook claims a system replaced a role, line two cannot walk that back. The reader hits the contradiction and stops.

Two shapes that work:

- **Resolve the objection.** Answer the doubt line one just created.
- **Before-state scene.** Drop the reader into the mess the claim came out of.

### Step 5. The single proof point

Name the one fact this post rests on. Then answer honestly: if somebody replies "prove it" in the comments, does it hold?

If not, the post needs a smaller claim or a different proof. Weak proof is not fixed by better writing.

### Step 6. Target length, with a reason tied to the goal

Working bands, as craft guidance rather than law: most posts land between 120 and 250 words. A story can earn up to 300. A lead post stays short, because the thing you are giving away is the payoff, not the post. Give the word count and the reason. "It feels right" is not a reason.

### Step 7. The prove-it list

List every claim in the idea that could not be defended if challenged. This is the most valuable part of the brief and the part everyone skips. For each claim: find the proof, soften it, or cut it.

## Output

Deliver the brief in this exact shape:

1. Goal, and what it ruled out, in one line
2. Hook family, and the one-sentence reason
3. Five hooks, with the recommendation
4. The second line, and which shape it uses
5. The proof point, and whether it holds
6. Target length and reason
7. The prove-it list, each claim resolved: proven, softened, or cut

## What good looks like

Hand the finished brief to someone who was not in the conversation and ask them to describe the post it would produce. If they land close to what you meant, the brief did its job. If the brief could describe ten different posts, the idea is still a topic, not a claim; rerun it with a sharper one.

## Guardrails

- Never hand over options without a recommendation.
- Never let line two change subject from, or contradict, line one.
- Never invent proof or numbers. Objective claims need evidence. A clearly framed opinion may proceed with a concrete example or observation.
- Never chase two goals in one post.
- One claim per post. If the prove-it list keeps finding new claims, the idea is two posts.
