# Example: voice-match

**This entire page is a worked example.** Dana, the firm, the clients, the posts, and every number are fictional, written to show what good input and output look like. Your own run uses your real writing.

**The founder:** Dana Reyes runs Ledgerline, a six-person bookkeeping firm serving owner-led small businesses. She has run it for eleven years.

## What Dana sent

> Use voice-match. Here are three of my posts.
>
> POST 1:
> A restaurant client swore he made money in March.
> His P&L said $6,200 profit. His bank account said overdraft.
>
> Took us two hours to find it. $9,800 of catering deposits for events that hadn't happened yet, all booked as revenue. Money he'd already spent on a new walk-in freezer.
>
> Those deposits were other people's money with a deadline attached.
>
> If your P&L and your gut disagree, believe neither. Reconcile.
>
> POST 2:
> Stop running your business off your bank balance.
>
> I've watched smart owners do it for eleven years. The balance looks healthy, so they buy the truck. Then payroll hits, then quarterly taxes hit, and suddenly they're on the phone with me at 9pm.
>
> Your bank balance is one number with no memory. It doesn't know about the $14,000 in unpaid invoices, and it definitely doesn't know what you owe the IRS.
>
> Current books beat instinct every month of the year. Boring, I know. So is making payroll on time.
>
> POST 3:
> We stopped billing by the hour in January. Moved all 34 clients to flat monthly pricing. One left.
>
> I rewrote the announcement email about ten times trying to soften it. My office manager finally said: just tell them what it costs now, and why.
>
> The client who left paid late, argued every invoice, and mailed us shoeboxes of receipts in April.
>
> Revenue per client is up 22% since. Invoice disputes are at zero.
>
> Charge for the work, not the clock. The first client to leave was the one the old pricing protected.

## What voice-match returned

```
VOICE PROFILE: Dana Reyes
Who I am: owner of a 6-person bookkeeping firm; I write for owner-led small businesses

Openings: a specific client moment or a blunt instruction in line one ("A restaurant client swore he made money in March.")
Rhythm: two or three plain sentences, then one short dry landing line ("Reconcile.")
Paragraphs: one to three lines each, white space between every thought
Punctuation: periods and commas; the samples do not use em dashes
Words I use: "the books", "reconcile", "payroll", "current", exact dollar figures
Words I never use: "hustle", "leverage", "game-changer", "journey"
Signature moves: exact unrounded numbers ($6,200, $9,800); translating accounting into consequences ("other people's money with a deadline attached"); admitting the wobble before the lesson ("I rewrote the announcement email about ten times")
Numbers: exact, never rounded; at least one real figure in every post
Humor: dry, one beat per post, always in the last two lines
Endings: one plain instruction or rule, never a motivational quote
Never do: exclamation points, emojis, "Agree?", pretending a loss didn't sting

Sounds exactly like me:
1. "Those deposits were other people's money with a deadline attached."
2. "Your bank balance is one number with no memory."
3. "Boring, I know. So is making payroll on time."
```

Along with the profile, the skill added one honest note: "Humor and Endings are based on only three samples. Paste two more posts when you have them and I will tighten those lines."

## Why this profile passes

Every line quotes or points to something visible in the three posts, and no line could describe half of LinkedIn. "Dry humor, one beat per post, always in the last two lines" picks Dana out of a lineup; "conversational but professional" would not. Dana saved the block in her notes app. It gets pasted into post-writer next: see post-writer-example.md.
