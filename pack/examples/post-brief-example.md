# Example: post-brief

Dana Reyes runs Ledgerline, a six-person bookkeeping firm serving owner-led small businesses. She has run it for eleven years. Dana, the firm, the clients, and every number in this example are fictional.

## What Dana pasted

> Idea: "We stopped billing hourly in January and moved every client to flat monthly pricing. Revenue per client went up 22% and invoice complaints went to zero. Two peers told me clients would leave. One did."
>
> Goal: Build trust with buyers.

## What post-brief returned

**1. Goal, and what it ruled out**

Build trust with buyers. That rules out a comment gate, a wide "pricing psychology" take, and any pitch at the end. This post is Dana's decision with the receipts, and the receipts do the selling.

**2. Hook family, and the reason**

First person. The entire value of this idea is that Dana made an unpopular call against direct advice and can show what happened. Corrective was the runner-up, but the belief being corrected ("hourly is safer") matters less than the lived result.

**3. Five hooks, with the recommendation**

1. "I moved all 34 clients off hourly billing in January. One left."
2. "Two peers said flat pricing would empty my client list. Revenue per client rose 22%."
3. "We killed hourly billing in January. Revenue per client is up 22%."
4. "Invoice complaints went to zero the month we stopped billing hours."
5. "34 clients moved to flat pricing. 33 stayed."

Recommendation: hook 1. It carries two numbers, ends on the loss instead of the win, and the loss is the gap. A reader has to know whether losing that client was a disaster or the plan.

**4. The second line, and its shape**

"The client who left was the exact one the old pricing was built around."

Shape: resolve the objection. Line one plants the doubt, losing clients is bad. Line two answers it, that loss was the model working, and stays on the same beat, the one who left.

**5. The proof point, and whether it holds**

The 22% rise in revenue per client since January, from Ledgerline's own billing records. Holds. It is Dana's own number and she can show the trend without exposing any client's name.

**6. Target length and reason**

180 to 220 words. A trust post earns room for the decision and the numbers, but this is one decision and one result, not a story arc. At 300 words it would be padding.

**7. The prove-it list**

- "Revenue per client up 22%": her own records. Proven, keep.
- "Invoice complaints went to zero": "zero" is an absolute. Dana confirmed zero disputes since January, so it stays, with the month named so the claim has edges.
- "Two peers told me clients would leave": private conversations, told as personal story, not as data. Fine as written.
- Implied claim, "flat pricing is better for clients": the numbers prove it was better for Ledgerline, not for clients. Cut it. The post says what happened, and lets readers draw that line themselves.
