# Example: hook-writer

Dana Reyes runs Ledgerline, a six-person bookkeeping firm serving owner-led small businesses. She has run it for eleven years. Dana, the firm, the clients, and every number in this example are fictional.

## What Dana pasted

> Idea: "A new client came in thinking their books were clean. In the first week we found $48,000 in unfiled sales tax across 3 states. Their old bookkeeper had never registered them anywhere."
>
> Extra details: "34 clients total, the cleanup took 6 weeks, the exposure covered 14 months of sales."

## What hook-writer returned

**1.** "I had to tell a new client they owed $48,000 the state didn't know about yet."
Family: first person. Why: money plus a human moment plus legal tension, and the gap is wide open. How does that conversation go, and how did it get this far?

**2.** "$48,000 in unfiled sales tax.
The client thought their books were clean."
Family: bare number first. Why: the number is startling alone, and line two adds the contradiction that makes it a story instead of a stat.

**3.** "Day 6 of onboarding a new client. My senior accountant called me over. She'd found something in the 2024 filings."
Family: unfinished story. Why: a real moment with the ending withheld. The reader has to know what she found.

**4.** "3 states. 14 months. $48,000. All missed by the last bookkeeper."
Family: bare number first. Why: the stacked numbers build scale, and the last beat redirects the gap to how a professional missed it.

**5.** "Clean books and compliant books are not the same thing. The gap cost one brand $48,000."
Family: corrective. Why: names a belief most founders quietly hold, then prices the gap. The body gets to explain the difference.

**6.** "Your books can look perfect and still be hiding a $48,000 problem."
Family: this X / your X. Why: moves the risk onto the reader. Weaker than the top five because it reads like a warning, not an event.

**7.** "Halfway through a routine onboarding, the cleanest client file we'd seen turned into a $48,000 problem."
Family: unfinished story. Why: the reversal carries it, but it spends more characters than hook 3 to open the same gap.

**8.** "We found $48,000 in unfiled sales tax in a client's first week with us."
Family: first person. Why: clear and true, but it resolves too much. The reader can guess the rest of the post.

**9.** "The most expensive bookkeeping mistakes never show up in the books."
Family: corrective. Why: a strong line with no number in it. Against the others it feels abstract.

**10.** "This $48,000 mistake was sitting in plain sight for 14 months."
Family: this X / your X. Why: "mistake" and "plain sight" are vague until the body explains, so the two-second clarity test barely passes.

**The pick:** hook 1. It survives both readings. A stranger knows exactly what is happening in two seconds, and cannot guess how it got this far or what happened next.

**What the body owes it:** the scene of that conversation, how the miss actually happened (never registered in any state), and what the 6-week cleanup took. No pitch. The competence is the pitch.
