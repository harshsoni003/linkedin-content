# Example: post-writer

**This entire page is a worked example.** Dana, Ledgerline, the client, and every number are fictional, written to show what good input and output look like. Your own run uses your real business.

**The founder:** Dana Reyes runs Ledgerline, a six-person bookkeeping firm serving owner-led small businesses. She has run it for eleven years. Her voice profile came from the voice-match example (see voice-match-example.md).

## What Dana sent

> Use post-writer.
> My goal: build trust with buyers.
> My voice profile:
>
> VOICE PROFILE: Dana Reyes
> Who I am: owner of a 6-person bookkeeping firm; I write for owner-led small businesses
> Openings: a specific client moment or a blunt instruction in line one ("A restaurant client swore he made money in March.")
> Rhythm: two or three plain sentences, then one short dry landing line ("Reconcile.")
> Paragraphs: one to three lines each, white space between every thought
> Punctuation: periods and commas; the samples do not use em dashes
> Words I use: "the books", "reconcile", "payroll", "current", exact dollar figures
> Words I never use: "hustle", "leverage", "game-changer", "journey"
> Signature moves: exact unrounded numbers ($6,200, $9,800); translating accounting into consequences ("other people's money with a deadline attached"); admitting the wobble before the lesson ("I rewrote the announcement email about ten times")
> Numbers: exact, never rounded; at least one real figure in every post
> Humor: dry, one beat per post, always in the last two lines
> Endings: one plain instruction or rule, never a motivational quote
> Never do: exclamation points, emojis, "Agree?", pretending a loss didn't sting
> Sounds exactly like me:
> 1. "Those deposits were other people's money with a deadline attached."
> 2. "Your bank balance is one number with no memory."
> 3. "Boring, I know. So is making payroll on time."
>
> The idea: A new client came in yesterday who hadn't reconciled in 14 months. Their bookkeeper was a cousin with a spreadsheet. Turns out they'd been paying for accounting software the whole time and nobody ever logged in.

## What post-writer returned

**The post**

> A new client sat down yesterday. Their books hadn't been reconciled in 14 months.
> Their bookkeeper was a cousin with a spreadsheet.
>
> Here's what 14 months of guessing looks like.
>
> No blame on the cousin. But a spreadsheet nobody checks against the bank is a diary with numbers in it.
>
> The part I can't get over: they were paying for real accounting software the entire time. [YOUR NUMBER: what it costs per month] a month. Nobody ever logged in.
>
> So for 14 months, pricing, hiring, and every "can we afford this" conversation ran on figures nobody had verified.
>
> The cleanup will take about [YOUR NUMBER: your honest estimate] weeks, mostly inside software they already own.
>
> Go open the accounting software you pay for. If you can't remember your login, you just learned something about your books.

**Fill in before posting**

1. [YOUR NUMBER: what it costs per month]: the monthly price of the software they never used. If you do not know it yet, cut that sentence rather than guessing.
2. [YOUR NUMBER: your honest estimate]: how many weeks the cleanup will really take. Underpromise here; this post is about trust.

**Three alternate openings**

1. Specific number with a twist:
> 14 months. That's how long this business ran with nobody checking the books.
> The bookkeeper was a cousin with a spreadsheet.

When to prefer it: your last few posts opened on scenes; this one leads with the number instead.

2. Contradiction of a belief:
> Free bookkeeping from family cost this client 14 months of flying blind.
> They were even paying for software that would have caught it.

When to prefer it: your audience skews toward owners who currently use a relative or friend for the books.

3. Scene in motion:
> Yesterday I opened a set of books last reconciled 14 months ago.
> The whole time, paid-for accounting software sat untouched. Zero logins.

When to prefer it: you want the quietest, most matter-of-fact open, useful when your recent posts ran hot.

**The call**

Run the original. "A cousin with a spreadsheet" is the strongest detail you gave, and it lands hardest as its own second line.

## What to notice

- Every fact came from Dana's three input sentences. The two numbers the post needed but she did not give became marked blanks instead of invented figures.
- The opening stays concise, and the question it opens (how bad is it, and what happens now) stays open past "see more."
- The line after "see more" is eight words and re-promises the payoff instead of dumping background.
- The ending follows Dana's profile (one plain instruction, dry beat at the close) and her goal. Build trust with buyers means showing how she thinks, so there is no pitch.
